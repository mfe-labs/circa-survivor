The contestWhat we are actually playing

Circa Survivor is a last-one-standing pool. About 25,000 entries paid in, and the prize pool is around $25 million.

  • There are 20 weeks. The 18 regular NFL weeks, plus two extra ones carved out for the Thanksgiving games and the Christmas games.
  • Each week you pick one team to win. No point spread. Just win.
  • Each team can only be used once all season. Spend the Chiefs in Week 2 and they are gone for good.
  • Lose, and you are out. A tie counts as a loss. Forgetting to pick counts as a loss.

Two things make it hard. First, you have to ration 20 picks across 32 teams, so a great team used carelessly is a great team wasted. Second, winning is not enough on its own: if you survive a week and so does everyone else, you have gained nothing. The prize goes to whoever is left, so you want to survive weeks that other people do not.

This dashboard tracks three entries, and every number on it exists to answer one of two questions: how likely is this team to win this week, and what does using them cost me later.

W% — the chance they actually winBuilt from betting odds, not from anyone's opinion

Sportsbooks post a price on each team. Those prices are the best public forecast that exists, because real money moves them. The catch is that a book's prices always add up to more than 100%, because the extra is the book's profit margin. So the number has to be cleaned up before it means anything.

Here is the whole process for one game.

WORKED EXAMPLE · Lions at Bills, Week 2

  1. DraftKings posts Bills −250 and Lions +205.
  2. Turn each price into a chance. "Bills −250" means risk $250 to win $100, so the implied chance is 250 ÷ 350 = 71.4%. "Lions +205" means risk $100 to win $205, so it is 100 ÷ 305 = 32.8%.
  3. Remove the book's cut. Those add to 104.2%. The extra 4.2% is the house edge. Scale both down so they add to exactly 100%: Bills 68.5%, Lions 31.5%.
  4. Do that for every book, separately. Pinnacle gives 68.2%, DraftKings 68.5%, BetMGM 68.9%, FanDuel 69.0%.
  5. Take the middle. With four numbers, that is the average of the middle two: 68.7%. The other side is whatever is left over, 31.3%.

The dashboard shows 69%.

Why the middle instead of the average? Because the middle ignores an outlier. If one book is slow to move its price after an injury, the average drags toward the stale number and the middle does not.

A game only gets a W% if a book has posted prices on both teams. Point spreads never produce a W%, and neither does any model. If nobody has posted a price yet, the cell is blank rather than filled with a guess.

The little amber dot next to a W% means fewer than three books were quoting that game, so the number rests on thin evidence. It is common early in the week and disappears as books post.

P% — how many other people are on the same teamThe thing that turns surviving into winning

If 8,000 entries pick the Jaguars and the Jaguars win, you survived, but so did 8,000 other people and you are no closer to the money. If you win a week that thins the field, you gain a lot of ground. So knowing what everyone else is doing matters as much as knowing who will win.

There are two cases.

After the week locks, guessing is unnecessary. Circa publishes a PDF listing every entry and its pick, and the dashboard reads it. In Week 1 the Jaguars were picked by 8,127 of 25,017 entries, so P% was exactly 32.5%.

Before the week locks, nobody publishes anything, so the app predicts the crowd with a small model. It gives each favorite a score from three ingredients and then turns the scores into percentages.

score = (win chance)a × e−b × future value × availability Win chance raised to a power, because the crowd piles onto the biggest favorite far harder than a straight proportion would suggest.
Future value pushes the score down, because the crowd saves teams that have easy games coming.
Availability is the share of surviving entries that have not already burned that team. A team most of the field has spent cannot be picked by them again.

The two knobs, a and b, are not chosen by hand. Every week that Circa's real numbers arrive, the app tries thousands of combinations and keeps whichever one came closest to what the crowd actually did, weighting the big picks more heavily than the tiny ones. Early in the season it is held near sensible starting values so one odd week cannot throw it off. It gets better with every week of real data.

How good is it? It gets the most-picked team within a point or two most weeks; the second favorite is where it misses, by 9 points on the Week 4 Ravens. When the week's Thursday game has kicked off, the app also uses a reading from Splash's survivor contest, a field the same size and price as Circa's that shows its Thursday picks immediately: the Thursday teams' shares blend what that crowd did, scaled up a little because Circa's crowd runs chalkier, with the model's own estimate, weighted by which has been more accurate so far; a Thursday result counts as decided. That is why P% switches to Circa's real numbers the moment they exist, and why before lock the tooltip on P%, EV and DILI shows a band like 38–45% next to the estimate. The band is the middle half of where the number could land, and it covers two things: the lines keep moving until Saturday, and the model itself, while it has nailed the most-picked team, has been off by a third or more on the runner-up. The line part narrows as lock approaches; the model part does not. Check the board Saturday morning before you lock.

Future — roughly how many good weeks a team has leftThe cost of spending someone

Using a team is permanent, so the real question is never "will they win this week" alone. It is "will I miss them later". The Future column answers that with one number: about how many strong-favorite weeks this team still has ahead of them that you would actually use.

For every week after the one you are looking at, the app checks how big a favorite the team projects to be, and counts that week partially. A week where they project at 75% counts as most of a week. A week at 65% counts as half. A week at 55% barely counts. Then it adds them up. That is the team's own count.

The column is per entry, though, because a strong week only matters to you if you would reach for that team in it. So the same weeks are counted again against the teams this entry still holds: full credit where the team would be your first or second option that week, less as third or fourth, none below. The number shown is a blend of the two counts, half and half early in the season and leaning toward your own cover as the season shortens, the same split DILI's forfeit uses. Spend your studs and the next team's weeks start counting for more.

WORKED EXAMPLE · Week 2

Ravens 6.8 — a stacked schedule ahead. Spending them now throws away a lot.
Bucs 2.0 — a couple of usable weeks left, and that is it.
Panthers 0.9 — essentially nothing later. If they are ever going to be useful, it is now.

That last line is the point of the column. The Panthers were a real Week 2 option precisely because nobody would miss them later, and the old version of this number could not tell them apart from the Bucs.

Where do the projections come from? Power ratings, one number per team, meaning "points better or worse than an average team". They are not opinions either. Every game this season that has a betting line becomes an equation, roughly home rating minus away rating plus two points for home field equals the spread, and the app solves all of them at once. Early in the year there are too few games to pin down 32 numbers, so the fit is anchored to the Super Bowl futures market: the odds bookmakers offer on each team to win the title, which is the market's direct verdict on who is actually good this season. That anchor loosens automatically as real games accumulate.

EV — is this a good pick for this week?Winning, measured against the field

EV combines the first two columns into one number. In words:

EV = your chance of surviving ÷ how crowded it will be if you do The bottom half is the share of the whole field expected to still be alive next week, assuming your team wins. It counts everyone who picked your team (they all survive alongside you), plus the expected survivors from every other game, and it counts your opponent's backers as gone.

The result is then rescaled so that 1.00 is what an average entry achieves. Above 1.00 means you gained ground on the field. Below means you lost ground, even if you won.

WORKED EXAMPLE · Two Week 2 options

49ers: 88% to win, but 33% of the field is on them. EV 1.11. Very likely to survive, and even after sharing the survival with a third of the field it is still the best play this week.

Bucs: 78% to win with 16% of the field on them. EV 1.00. Lower win chance, but far fewer people riding along, so it comes out level with average.

A team can have a high win chance and a mediocre EV. That is the whole idea: being right alongside everybody else is worth very little.

If some games have no prices yet, EV is computed from the ones that do, and the column header shows EV* with the coverage in its tooltip. Below 75% of games priced, EV is left blank rather than shown misleadingly, because missing teams quietly flatter everyone else.

DILI — "do I love it?"The one column that answers the actual question

EV is about this week only. It has no idea that the team you are about to burn is your best Week 16 option. DILI fixes that: it takes EV and then charges you for what you are giving up.

DILI = EV ÷ forfeit Same scale as EV, so 1.00 still means "as good as an average entry does". DILI is calculated separately for each of your entries, because it depends on which teams that entry has already used.

Your map

For each entry the app works out a map: the best way to fill every remaining week, Thanksgiving and Christmas included, with a different team the entry still holds, so that the chance of winning all of them is as high as possible. The Map tab shows it week by week. The forfeit for a team is what the map loses when you burn that team and re-solve. Usually that is one swap: the week the team was going to cover falls to the next-best team left for it. A team that is not on your map costs little to spend on that score. But the map assumes the plan holds, and plans don't, so the forfeit also charges for broad strength: a team with several strong spots ahead costs more to spend than one with none, even if the map happens not to need it. That strength part counts for half early in the season and fades to nothing by the end.

The holidays need no special rule. The map must put an eligible team on Thanksgiving (10 teams can play it) and on Christmas (8 can), so burning one of those teams is charged what it costs there, and burning your last eligible one makes the map impossible: DILI reads 0.00.

WORKED EXAMPLE · Ravens vs Vikings, Week 4

Both about 84% to win. Ravens EV 1.10 (18% of the field on them), Vikings EV 1.00 (63%).

The Ravens are on this entry's map at Week 16, home to Cleveland at 83%. Burn them and the Chargers cover that week at 70%. Forfeit 1.13, so DILI = 1.10 ÷ 1.13 = 0.97.

The Vikings are not on the map. Forfeit 1.06, DILI = 0.95. A coin flip, and the column says so.

Why the forfeit is not the whole 13 points

A Week 16 line projected in September is a guess. Measured over seven seasons, projections like these miss the closing spread by about 3 points a month out and 6 points by December. So the map is solved 96 times with every future line jiggled by that much, and the forfeit is the average. In some of those seasons someone else is the best Week 16 option and the Ravens cost nothing; in others they are the only good one and cost a lot. That is also why holding two decent Christmas teams is worth more than one great one.

The next four weeks go one step further and use each team's projected EV rather than its win chance, by running the pick-popularity model forward. A team most of the field burns this week becomes a quiet, valuable pick later, and the map knows it.

The Map tabThe rest of the season, one row per week

Pick an entry and the Map tab lays out its whole season two ways, each on its own tab. Weeks already played are grayed out with the result.

Claude's DILI Map is the Planner's rule applied forward: score the next open week exactly as the Planner does, take the top DILI, spend that team, move on, repeat to the end, as if nothing changes. Its current week is always the Planner's pick. Each row shows the pick, its DILI, the second-best team with its DILI, and whether the call was close. Weeks beyond the next few have no field model yet, so they score on win chance against the forfeit, and the row says so.

Claude's 96 Map is the plan the jiggled seasons agree on. The season is solved 96 times with every future line nudged by its usual error, each team's count per week is tallied, and the plan is assembled in order of agreement so no team is used twice. "Held 29/96" means 29 of the 96 seasons put that team there; the backup is the runner-up by count. This tab changes slowly and tells you where the plan is sturdy and where it is a toss-up.

The two usually agree. They split on toss-up weeks, where the DILI map commits to one pick and the 96 map shows a low count, and that split is the information: use the DILI map to decide, the 96 map to see how sure the plan is.

Across the top: the chance of winning every week on the map shown, its three weakest weeks, and the cheapest favorites to burn this week (on the DILI map, by what spending them costs this entry; on the 96 map, by how rarely a later week needs them).

Your own maps

The tabs above the three boxes switch between Claude's Map and any maps you have saved, and the boxes follow the tab. The + tab starts a new map as a copy of Claude's DILI Map; name it and change any week with the small arrow next to the pick, which lists that week's teams by win chance. Teams already used elsewhere are crossed out but still allowed, and both weeks are then marked as a conflict until one changes. Saved maps are shared, and win chances update with the lines. Only the signed-in owner can create or edit them.

Reading the gridWhat every colour and mark means

The five number columns on the left describe the week you have selected. Everything to the right is the schedule: each team's opponent every week, with the point spread underneath.

In the schedule

  • vs CLEYour pick for that week, for the entry you are currently planning.
  • @ CINStruck through means the team is already used, so that cell is unavailable.
  • @ SEAA greener cell means a bigger favorite. Deeper green, safer game.
  • −8.5A real point spread from a sportsbook.
  • −2.5Italic and faded means there is no betting line yet, so this is projected from power ratings. Projections never feed W%.
  • ¹A small raised number means one of your other entries also has that team that week.

Next to a team name

  • Plays on Thanksgiving week.
  • Plays on Christmas week. Two dots means both, and those are the six teams that pay the double holiday dock.

In the number columns

  • 1.11Green means one of the five best in that column. In the Future column green means 2.0 or below, meaning cheap to burn.
  • 33%Red P% means more than about a tenth of the field is on that team. A crowded pick.
  • 69% An amber dot means fewer than three books were quoting that game.
  • +.01The small number beside a value is how much it moved since the previous refresh. Green up, red down.
  • 2The blue wash and frame mark the week you are planning and the entry you are planning it for.

Hovering almost anything gives the detail behind it, including the full arithmetic behind a DILI score. The Model details button opens every number for the week, book by book and team by team.

The Actuals tabHow the whole contest is going

The Planner is about what to do next. The Actuals tab is about what already happened, to all 25,000 entries.

  • Entries alive and how many were knocked out.
  • Implied value per entry: the prize pool divided by the number of entries still standing. It climbs every week as people are eliminated.
  • Your equity: that value multiplied by your share of the entries still alive.
  • The week-by-week breakdown: exactly how many entries took each team, whether it won or lost, how many were eliminated by it, and what the popularity model had predicted beforehand. That last column is how you judge whether the model is any good.

Week 1 is a good illustration of the whole game. The Chargers were the second most popular pick at 7,585 entries. They lost to the Cardinals, and a third of the entire field was gone in an afternoon.

A game is never overwritten once it has kicked off, so each one keeps the last price seen before kickoff. Every save and every automatic update is a permanent record, so the entire history of picks, odds and results is recoverable.

What it can't doWorth knowing before you trust a number

  • Distant weeks are rough. Next week's games usually have a look-ahead spread, which the app uses. Anything further out has no betting line anywhere free, so it is projected from power ratings that are deliberately cautious. Treat a Future value of 4.4 as "several good weeks", not as a precise count.
  • The map is a plan, not a prediction. It is re-solved every refresh from whatever the lines and ratings say that day, and it will change. Use it to see what a pick costs, not as a list of future picks.
  • It only guesses the crowd four weeks ahead. Pick popularity is projected forward that far; beyond it the field model drifts, so distant weeks use win chance alone.
  • Circa's own prices are not available. The contest is run by a sportsbook whose odds are not published through any free feed, so W% comes from five other books.
  • It has no opinion about people. No injuries, no weather, no narratives. If a story matters, the betting market has already priced it, and if it has not, neither has this.
